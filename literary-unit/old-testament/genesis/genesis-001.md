## Genre

Genesis 1 is **elevated prose narrative**. It is not Hebrew poetry in the strict sense (it lacks consistent line-by-line parallelism), but it is so rhythmic, repetitive, and carefully numbered that it reads almost like liturgy. The style is calm and declarative, with no conflict, dialogue partners, or drama, only command and fulfillment.

## Boundaries of the Unit

The unit is **1:1–2:3**, not chapter 1 alone. It is framed by an **inclusio**: "God created the heaven and the earth" (1:1) is answered by "the heavens and the earth were finished" and "which God created" (2:1–3). Genesis 2:4a ("These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth") either closes this account or opens the next. It is the first of the *toledot* ("generations") formulas that organize the whole book.

## Structure: Forming and Filling

Verse 2 names two problems: the earth is *formless* (*tohu*) and *empty* (*bohu*). The six days answer them in two matching sets of three:

- **Day 1** (light and darkness) is filled on **Day 4** (sun, moon, stars)
- **Day 2** (sky and waters) is filled on **Day 5** (birds and sea creatures)
- **Day 3** (dry land and plants) is filled on **Day 6** (land animals and humans)

Days 1–3 **form** the realms, and days 4–6 **fill** them.

Days 3 and 6 each contain **two** creative acts, so each triad ends with a double climax. **Day 7** stands outside the pattern entirely: nothing is made, and God rests and sanctifies the day.

## The Refrains

Each day is built from a recurring set of formulas, which makes small variations stand out:

1. **Announcement:** "And God said…"
2. **Command:** "Let there be…" / "Let the waters…"
3. **Fulfillment:** "and it was so"
4. **Action or naming:** God divides, makes, or calls
5. **Evaluation:** "God saw that it was good"
6. **Time marker:** "the evening and the morning were the … day"

**Notable departures:**

- Day 2 lacks "it was good."
- Day 6 escalates to "very good" (v. 31).
- Day 7 has no evening-and-morning refrain, which leaves God's rest open-ended.
- Naming happens in days 1–3, and **blessing** in days 5, 6, and 7.

## Patterns of Seven and Ten

The chapter is built around the number **seven**, the number of completion:

- v. 1 has **7** Hebrew words, and v. 2 has **14** (2 × 7).
- "God" (*Elohim*) occurs **35** times in 1:1–2:3 (5 × 7).
- "Good" occurs **7** times (vv. 4, 10, 12, 18, 21, 25, 31).
- The whole account culminates on the **seventh** day.

"And God said" occurs **ten** times (vv. 3, 6, 9, 11, 14, 20, 24, 26, 28, 29). Jewish tradition speaks of the world being created by "ten utterances" (Pirkei Avot 5:1).

## The Climax: Verse 27

The pace changes at v. 26. The impersonal "Let there be" gives way to a deliberative **"Let us make,"** and v. 27 breaks into a short **poetic triplet**, the most poetic moment of the chapter. The verb *bara* ("created") appears three times in a single verse:

- created man in his own image
- in the image of God created he him
- male and female created he them

The first two lines form a small **chiasm** (created / image // image / created). The third line adds a new element: humankind is male and female together.

## Key Word Patterns

- **"Divide/separate"** (vv. 4, 6, 7, 14, 18): creation as ordering by separation.
- **"After his kind"** (10 times in vv. 11–25): order and stability in living things.
- **"Light"/"darkness"**: introduced in day 1 and given rulers in day 4, which ties the two triads together.

## How the Chapter Reads as a Whole

The movement is from chaos to order, from emptiness to fullness, and from darkness to light. It ends not with humankind but with **rest and holiness**. The structure carries the theology: a sovereign God creates by word, the world is purposeful and good, humans are set within it as God's image-bearers, and the week culminates in sacred time.
