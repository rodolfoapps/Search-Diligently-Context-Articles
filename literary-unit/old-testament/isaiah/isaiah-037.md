## Genre

Isaiah 37 is **prophetic historical narrative**: prose storytelling built around three embedded speeches. Two are oracles from Isaiah and one is a royal prayer. The longest oracle (vv. 22–29) is **poetry**, a taunt song directed at an enemy king. The chapter mixes three genres:

- **Narrative** (vv. 1–2, 5, 8–9, 14–15, 21, 36–38), including messenger speeches (vv. 3–4, 10–13)
- **Prayer** (vv. 16–20)
- **Prophetic poetry and oracle** (vv. 6–7, 22–35)

## The Larger Unit

Chapter 37 is the second half of a single story that begins at 36:1 and ends at 37:38. Chapter 36 sets up the threat: the Rabshakeh's speech asks, in effect, "On whom do you trust?" Chapter 37 answers with the LORD's response. The block of chapters 36–39 sits between the Assyrian-era prophecies (chs. 1–35) and the Babylonian-era prophecies (chs. 40–66), and works as a hinge between them.

## A Double Cycle

The chapter tells the same pattern **twice**:

- **Cycle 1 (36:1–37:7):** Assyrian threat delivered by speech → Hezekiah goes to the temple → envoys sent to Isaiah → short oracle
- **Cycle 2 (37:8–35):** Assyrian threat delivered by letter → Hezekiah goes to the temple and prays himself → Isaiah sends a long oracle

Since Stade (1886), many scholars have explained this doubling as two originally separate accounts, often labeled "B1" and "B2," woven together. Read as it stands, the repetition creates **escalation**. The threat moves from spoken word to written document. Hezekiah moves from sending others to pray to praying himself. God's answer grows from three verses to fifteen.

## Prophecy and Fulfillment Frame

Isaiah's first oracle (v. 7) makes three predictions: Sennacherib will hear a rumor, return to his land, and fall by the sword there. The chapter's last verses (vv. 37–38) fulfill each one in order. This **prophecy-fulfillment bracket** frames everything in between.

## Hezekiah's Prayer (vv. 16–20)

The prayer follows a classic shape:

1. **Invocation** naming God as enthroned above the cherubim, the only God, and Creator (v. 16)
2. **Petition to hear and see** (v. 17)
3. **Acknowledgment of the facts:** the conquered nations' gods were "no gods" but wood and stone (vv. 18–19)
4. **Petition with purpose:** "save us… that all the kingdoms of the earth may know" (v. 20)

The prayer answers Assyria's argument directly. Assyria had said that the gods of the nations could not save them. Hezekiah agrees, because those were not real gods, and asks the living God to prove the difference.

## The Taunt Song (vv. 22–29)

- **Personification:** Jerusalem is the *virgin daughter of Zion* who laughs and shakes her head at the invader. The besieged city is pictured as an unconquered young woman scorning a would-be assailant.
- **Quoting the enemy:** vv. 24–25 put Sennacherib's boasts in his own mouth (chariots on mountain heights, cutting cedars of Lebanon, drying up rivers). The images closely match the style of real Assyrian royal inscriptions.
- **Reversal:** God's reply (vv. 26–29) recasts the king's conquests as God's long-planned purpose. The proud king becomes a beast led by a **hook in the nose and a bridle in the lips**, a fitting reversal, since Assyrian art shows captives led by ropes and rings.
- **Meter:** Much of the poem uses the uneven, "limping" 3+2 rhythm (*qinah*) associated with laments, turned here into mockery.

## Key Words and Motifs

- **"Reproach"** (vv. 4, 17, 23, 24): Assyria's taunting of the living God is the charge God answers.
- **"Living God" vs. "no gods"** (vv. 4, 17, 19): the central theological contrast.
- **"Hand"**: "deliver… out of the hand of" Assyria (36:15, 18–20; 37:20) and gods that were the work of men's hands (v. 19).
- **"The way he came"** (vv. 29, 34): repeated to promise a complete reversal of the invasion route.
- **"Remnant"** (vv. 4, 31, 32): what Hezekiah prays for in v. 4 is what God promises in vv. 31–32.

## An Echo of Isaiah 7

The Rabshakeh stands by "the conduit of the upper pool" on the road to the fuller's field (36:2). This is exactly where Isaiah had confronted Hezekiah's father **Ahaz** decades earlier (7:3). Ahaz refused to trust God and sought Assyria's help; Hezekiah, at the same spot, turns to the LORD. The book invites readers to compare the two kings.
