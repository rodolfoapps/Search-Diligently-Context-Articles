## Overview

God speaks, and a dark, empty, water-covered world becomes ordered and full. Over six days he makes light, sky, land, and plants, then fills those spaces with lights, birds and fish, animals, and finally humankind, male and female, made in his image and blessed to rule the earth. Each day closes with the same refrain, and seven times God sees that what he made is good.

The account ends at 2:3, not 1:31. The chapter break came centuries later, and it cuts off the seventh day, when God rests and makes the day holy. Read with 2:1–3 attached, the story ends in rest rather than in the creation of man.

---

## Historical Context

- **Author:** Not named in the text. Jewish and Christian tradition credits Moses. Latter-day Saint scripture goes further: in Moses 1–2 the account comes to Moses by revelation, with the command to write it (Moses 1:40; 2:1). Many scholars assign it to a later priestly writer drawing on older material.
- **When revealed (Restoration):** Moses 1 sets the vision after the burning bush and before Israel's deliverance from Egypt (Moses 1:17, 25–26).
- **Date written:** Unknown. Tradition places it in Moses' lifetime (15th or 13th century BC); scholarly proposals reach the 6th–5th century BC.
- **Date of events:** Biblical chronology places Adam about 4000 BC. Archbishop Ussher's 1650 reckoning, long printed in KJV margins, gives 4004 BC. D&C 77:6 speaks of the earth's seven thousand years of "temporal existence."
- **Language:** Biblical Hebrew.
- **First audience:** Israel, whether in the wilderness (traditional view) or in exile in Babylon (common scholarly view).
- **Parallel accounts:** Genesis 2:4–25 (a second creation account), Moses 2, Abraham 4.

## Hermeneutics

Babylon's creation epic, *Enuma Elish*, has the same pieces as Genesis 1: primeval waters, a sky that holds water back above, land below. There the god Marduk gets them by killing the sea goddess Tiamat and splitting her body in half, and humans are made from a slain god's blood to do the gods' work. Genesis keeps the pieces and drops the war. The "deep" (*tehom*, a word related to Tiamat's name) is just water. God gives an order and it is done, and the humans he makes are blessed and fed (vv. 28–29), not put to work for him.

On day four the sun and moon go unnamed. They are "the greater light" and "the lesser light." Across the region the sun was a god (Babylon's Shamash, Egypt's Ra), and the common Hebrew word for sun, *shemesh*, matches the Babylonian god's name. Genesis also makes them on day four, after light already exists on day one. A listener raised among sun worshipers would hear a demotion: these are lamps hung up to keep the calendar.

"Image of God" was royal language. Egyptian and Assyrian texts call the king the image of a god, and kings set up statues of themselves in conquered lands to mark whose land it was. Genesis gives that title to every human, male and female, and the rule it grants is over animals and the earth, not over other people.

## Literary Structure

**Type:** Parallel panels (A B C / A′ B′ C′) inside a frame. Not a chiasm.

```
FRAME   1:1    God created the heaven and the earth
  A  Day 1  Light ............... A′ Day 4  Lights in the sky
  B  Day 2  Sky and waters ...... B′ Day 5  Birds and sea creatures
  C  Day 3  Land and plants ..... C′ Day 6  Animals and humankind
FRAME   2:1–3  Heaven and earth finished — Day 7, rest
```

Verse 2 names two problems: the earth is formless (*tohu*) and empty (*bohu*). Days 1–3 solve the first by making spaces. Days 4–6 solve the second by filling them. Day 7 sits outside both panels; nothing is made.

**Repetition to watch:** "And God said" (10 times), "it was so," "God saw that it was good" (7 times, missing on day 2, raised to "very good" on day 6), "the evening and the morning." Day 7 has no evening-and-morning line.

**Sevens:** Verse 1 has 7 Hebrew words and verse 2 has 14. "God" (*Elohim*) appears 35 times in 1:1–2:3.

**Small chiasm in v. 27:**
> A created man
>  B in his own image,
>  B′ in the image of God
> A′ created he him

## Literary Unit

**Where it sits:**

```
GENESIS
[ Primeval history 1–11 ][ Abraham 11:27–25 ][ Jacob 25–36 ][ Joseph 37–50 ]
        │
        ├─ ▶ Creation 1:1–2:3   ← you are here
        ├─ Eden and after 2:4–4:26
        ├─ Adam to Noah 5
        ├─ The Flood 6–9
        └─ Nations and Babel 10–11
```

Genesis is organized by headings that read "these are the generations of…" (Hebrew *toledot*). The first one is 2:4, so 1:1–2:3 stands in front of all of them as a prologue to the book. It opens the primeval history (chapters 1–11), which runs from creation to the scattering at Babel before narrowing to one family, Abraham's.

**Echoes later in the Torah:** The seventh day becomes the Sabbath command, grounded in creation (Exodus 20:11). When Moses finishes the tabernacle, the wording echoes 2:1–3: the work is finished and Moses blesses it (Exodus 39:32, 43).

## Traditions

How early readers received this chapter. These are traditions, not history; each is labeled by who held it.

**Restoration**

- **Moses 1–2 (revealed 1830).** In the Restoration account, Genesis 1 is not a stand-alone text but part of a larger vision. Moses sees the earth and every soul on it, asks why these things are so and by what God made them, and is told that God has made "worlds without number" through the Only Begotten (Moses 1:27–33). The creation account follows as the answer, introduced with "write the words which I speak" (Moses 2:1). The Lord also warns that men will take words from Moses' book and promises to raise up one like Moses to restore them (Moses 1:40–41), which Latter-day Saints read as Joseph Smith.
- **Abraham 4–5 (published 1842).** A second Restoration version told as a plan. "The Gods" take counsel, organize, and form; each "day" is a "time" (Abraham 4:8), and in 4:18 the Gods "watched those things which they had ordered until they obeyed." Abraham 3:4 sets the Lord's reckoning at a thousand years to a day. Where Genesis reports what happened, Abraham describes what was planned and then carried out (Abraham 4:31; 5:1–5).
- **Joseph Smith, King Follett sermon (April 1844).** He argued that the Hebrew *bara* means to organize, "the same as a man would organize materials and build a ship," and that element itself was never created. He also read the opening words as a council of the Gods, which matches the plural "Let us" of v. 26 and Abraham 4:26.

**Jewish**

- **Book of Jubilees (2nd century BC).** A retelling of Genesis framed as dictation from the angel of the presence to Moses on Sinai, the same setting Moses 1 gives. It fills in what Genesis leaves out: the angels, the spirits over wind, cloud, and snow, and the deep were all created on the first day (2:2). It counts 22 works of creation and matches them to the 22 generations from Adam to Jacob (2:23), so creation and Israel's family line have the same shape. The Sabbath, Jubilees says, was first kept by God and the highest angels before any human kept it.
- **Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC–AD 50).** A Jewish philosopher in Egypt who read Genesis through Greek philosophy. He says God did not need six days, since he could make everything at once. Six is used because it is the first "perfect" number, the sum of its factors (1 + 2 + 3), and order requires number (*On the Creation* 13). He reads the man of 1:27 as an ideal, spiritual pattern and the man of 2:7 as the earthly one, which resembles Moses 3:5's spiritual-then-natural creation.
- **Josephus (c. AD 93).** Opens his *Antiquities* with a plain retelling of creation, then stops on one detail: Moses wrote "one day," not "the first day." Josephus says he could explain why but saves it for a later book on causes that he never wrote (*Antiquities* 1.1.1).
- **Mishnah (c. AD 200).** The rabbis treated the "work of creation" (*ma'aseh bereshit*) as guarded knowledge, taught to no more than one student at a time (*Hagigah* 2:1). The same passage warns against asking what is above, below, before, and after. *Pirkei Avot* 5:1 asks why the world was made with ten sayings ("And God said") when one would have done, and answers that it raises the cost of destroying the world and the reward for sustaining it.
- **Babylonian Talmud, Hagigah 12a (c. AD 500).** Lists ten things created on the first day, including light, darkness, wind, water, and the measure of day and night. It also explains the light of day one, which exists before the sun: with that light Adam could see from one end of the world to the other, but God hid it away for the righteous in the world to come.
- **Genesis Rabbah (c. AD 400–500).** Reads the wisdom of Proverbs 8 ("I was by him, as one brought up with him," 8:30) as the Torah, and says God looked into the Torah and created the world from it, the way an architect builds from plans (1:1). Creation follows a written design that existed before the world.
- **Rashi (11th century).** His commentary opens by asking why the Torah begins with creation instead of with Israel's first commandment (Exodus 12). His answer: so that if the nations accuse Israel of taking Canaan, Israel can reply that the whole earth is God's and he gives land to whom he pleases.

**Early Christian**

- **Epistle of Barnabas (c. AD 100).** Reads the six days as prophecy. With the Lord a day is a thousand years, so in six thousand years he will bring all things to an end, and the seventh day is the rest that follows his coming (Barnabas 15). D&C 77:6–7, 12 uses the same seven-thousand-year frame for the earth's temporal history.
- **Theophilus of Antioch (c. AD 180).** Sees the three days before the sun and moon as a type of God, his Word, and his Wisdom, and uses the word *Trias* for it, the earliest known use of the term (*To Autolycus* 2.15). He also notes that light comes before the sun so no one would worship the sun as its source.
- **Basil of Caesarea (c. AD 370).** Takes up the same detail as Josephus. Scripture says "one day" because the day stands alone and points to eternity, the single day without evening (*Hexaemeron* 2.8). His sermons on the six days were the most read Christian commentary on Genesis 1 for centuries.
- **Augustine (c. AD 401–415).** Argued the days are not days like ours, since the sun does not exist until day four. He proposed that God created all things at once, and the six days teach the order of creation to minds that understand things in sequence (*The Literal Meaning of Genesis* 4–5). He also warned Christians against defending readings of Genesis that conflict with what people can plainly observe of the world (1.19).

**Other ancient**

- **Berossus (c. 290 BC).** A Babylonian priest who wrote his people's history in Greek. His creation account survives only in quotes by later writers. In it, Bel cuts the sea-woman Thalatth in two, making the heaven from one half and the earth from the other, then has his own head cut off so the gods can mix his blood with earth to make humans. It shows the Babylonian story that Genesis 1 stands against (see Hermeneutics).
