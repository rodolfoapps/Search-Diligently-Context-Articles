## Where This Chapter Fits

Isaiah 36–39 is a block of **prose narrative** set among chapters of prophetic poetry. It closes the first major part of the book (chs. 1–39, set in the 8th century BC). Chapters 36–37 tell of Assyria's threat to Jerusalem and the city's deliverance. Chapters 38–39 tell of Hezekiah's illness and the visit of envoys from Babylon, which looks ahead to the Babylonian setting of chapters 40–66. Chapter 37 is the resolution of the crisis that chapter 36 sets up: the Assyrian field commander has just stood outside Jerusalem's walls and mocked Hezekiah's trust in the LORD.

These same events appear almost word for word in **2 Kings 18:13–19:37** and in shorter form in **2 Chronicles 32**. Scholars debate which version is earlier. Most think the Isaiah form was adapted from (or shares a source with) the Kings account.

## Date and Historical Setting: 701 BC

This is one of the best-documented events in the Old Testament.

- **The empire:** Assyria, ruled from Nineveh, dominated the Near East. In 722 BC it had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel (Samaria).
- **The rebellion:** After the Assyrian king Sargon II died in 705 BC, **Hezekiah** of Judah joined a coalition withholding tribute, with hope of Egyptian support. He prepared Jerusalem for siege, including a water tunnel (2 Kings 20:20) that still survives, along with the Siloam Inscription carved inside it.
- **The campaign:** In **701 BC**, King **Sennacherib** (reigned 705–681 BC) marched west on his "third campaign." His own annals, preserved on clay prisms, claim he captured **46** of Hezekiah's fortified cities, deported many people, and shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage." The annals record heavy tribute (compare 2 Kings 18:14–16) but **do not claim that Jerusalem was taken**, which is unusual for Assyrian royal boasting.
- **Lachish:** The siege of Lachish, Judah's second city (see v. 8), was carved in stone reliefs on Sennacherib's palace walls at Nineveh, now in the British Museum. Excavations at Lachish confirm a violent destruction at this time.

**A dating puzzle:** Isaiah 36:1 places the invasion in Hezekiah's **14th year**. If 701 BC was his 14th year, he came to the throne around 715 BC. That date conflicts with other synchronisms in 2 Kings 18:1, 9–10, which suggest an earlier accession around 727 BC. Proposed solutions include a co-regency with his father Ahaz or a copying error.

## People and Places

- **Hezekiah:** king of Judah, praised in 2 Kings 18:5 for unmatched trust in the LORD. A clay seal impression reading "belonging to Hezekiah [son of] Ahaz, king of Judah" was found in excavations in Jerusalem and published in 2015.
- **Isaiah son of Amoz:** prophet in Jerusalem, active c. 740–700 BC, with access to the royal court.
- **Eliakim and Shebna** (v. 2): senior court officials. Isaiah 22:15–25 records an oracle demoting Shebna and elevating Eliakim.
- **Rabshakeh:** not a personal name but an Assyrian title (*rab-šāqê*, roughly "chief cupbearer"), a high-ranking official.
- **Tirhakah, "king of Ethiopia"** (v. 9): Taharqa of the Nubian (Kushite) 25th Dynasty, which ruled Egypt. He became king in 690 BC, so in 701 he was likely a prince or military commander; the narrator uses the title he later held. Assyrian records confirm a battle against an Egyptian-Kushite force at Eltekeh during this campaign.
- **Gozan, Haran, Rezeph, Eden, Hamath, Arpad, Sepharvaim, Hena, Ivah** (vv. 12–13): cities and peoples previously conquered by Assyria, cited to argue that no god had ever stopped Assyria.
- **Nisroch** (v. 38): an unidentified Assyrian god. The name may be a garbled form of Nusku or Ninurta.

## The Outcome (vv. 36–38)

The text attributes Assyria's withdrawal to the **angel of the LORD** striking 185,000 men in the camp. No Assyrian source mentions a disaster, which is unsurprising because royal annals did not record defeats. The Greek historian **Herodotus** (5th century BC) preserves an Egyptian tradition that mice swarmed Sennacherib's army and gnawed their bowstrings, which some read as a memory of plague. However the event is explained, the historical facts agree: Sennacherib went home, and Jerusalem was not captured.

Verse 38 compresses about **20 years**. Sennacherib was assassinated in **681 BC** by a son. Babylonian and Assyrian sources confirm the murder and that another son, **Esarhaddon**, succeeded him. Assyriologists identify "Adrammelech" with the crown prince Arda-Mulissu (Urad-Mullissu).

## Theological Setting

The episode became central to **Zion theology**, the conviction that God would defend Jerusalem "for mine own sake, and for my servant David's sake" (v. 35; see the Davidic covenant in 2 Samuel 7). Over a century later, that confidence hardened into a belief that the temple guaranteed the city's safety. Jeremiah challenged that assumption (Jer. 7; 26), and Lehi was among the prophets who warned that Jerusalem would fall (1 Nephi 1:4, 13, 18–20) before Babylon destroyed the city in 586 BC.

## Restoration Connections

- The **brass plates** carried by Lehi's family contained Isaiah's writings, and Nephi quotes Isaiah extensively (1 Nephi 20–21; 2 Nephi 7–8, 12–24). Isaiah 36–39 is not among the chapters quoted.
- Nephi explains that Isaiah is hard to understand without knowing "the manner of prophesying among the Jews" and "the regions round about" (2 Nephi 25:1–6). This chapter, full of places, rulers, and diplomatic exchanges, shows why that historical knowledge matters.
- The promise that a **remnant** of Judah would "take root downward, and bear fruit upward" (vv. 31–32) anticipates a theme the Book of Mormon develops at length: the preservation and future gathering of a remnant of Israel.
