## Where This Chapter Fits

Genesis 1 opens the Bible, the Torah (the five books of Moses), and the **primeval history** of Genesis 1–11, which moves from creation to the scattering of nations before narrowing to Abraham in chapter 12. The literary unit actually runs from **1:1 to 2:3**: the seventh day belongs with the first six, and the chapter break after 1:31 was added in the Middle Ages (chapter divisions are usually credited to Stephen Langton, c. AD 1220s). A second, differently styled creation account begins at 2:4.

## Authorship and Date

The chapter itself names no author and gives no date.

- **Traditional view:** Jewish and Christian tradition attribute the Torah to Moses (13th or 15th century BC, depending on the dating of the Exodus).
- **Critical scholarship:** many scholars assign 1:1–2:3 to a "Priestly" (P) writer or school, usually dated to the exile or just after (6th–5th century BC), drawing on older traditions. Others argue for earlier dates or for a more unified composition. These remain hypotheses; no manuscript evidence directly settles the question.
- **Restoration perspective:** Moses 1–2 (revealed to Joseph Smith in 1830 during his inspired revision of the Bible) presents this creation account as a revelation given to Moses, framed by the Lord's words "write the words which I speak" (Moses 2:1). Abraham 4 offers a parallel account in which "the Gods" plan and organize the creation.

## Original Language and Key Terms

Genesis was written in **Biblical Hebrew**. A few words shape how the chapter is read:

- ***Bereshit*** ("In the beginning," v. 1) is the Hebrew name of the book. The word lacks the definite article, so v. 1 can be read as an independent statement ("In the beginning God created…") or as a time clause ("When God began to create…," as in the NRSV and JPS translations). The choice affects whether v. 2 describes a state *before* God's first act.
- ***Bara*** ("create," vv. 1, 21, 27) is used in the Hebrew Bible only with God as subject. Many Hebrew scholars note that the verb itself does not require creation out of nothing. Joseph Smith taught that the word means "to organize" existing material (King Follett Discourse, 1844), and Abraham 4 consistently uses "organized" and "formed."
- ***Elohim*** ("God") is plural in form but takes singular verbs here; it is the only divine name used in 1:1–2:3.
- ***Tohu wa-bohu*** ("without form, and void," v. 2) is a rhyming pair describing an empty, unproductive state; the rest of the chapter answers it.
- ***Tehom*** ("the deep," v. 2) is the primordial ocean. It is linguistically related to *Tiamat*, the sea-goddess of Babylonian myth, but in Genesis it is simply water, not a rival deity.
- ***Ruach elohim*** (v. 2) can mean "Spirit of God" or "wind of God"; *ruach* means breath, wind, and spirit.
- ***Raqia*** ("firmament," v. 6) comes from a verb meaning to beat or hammer out (like metal). It describes a solid-seeming dome or expanse separating waters above from waters below, as ancient Israelites perceived the sky. Abraham 4:6 renders it "expanse."
- ***Tselem*** and ***demut*** ("image" and "likeness," v. 26).

## The Ancient Near Eastern Setting

Genesis 1 was first heard in a world full of creation stories, and much of its meaning comes from how it differs from them.

- **Babylon:** In *Enuma Elish*, the god Marduk defeats the sea-goddess Tiamat and builds heaven and earth from her split corpse; humans are made from a slain god's blood to do the gods' labor. Genesis also separates waters above and below, but with no battle: God speaks, and it happens. Humans are not slaves but royal representatives who receive blessing (v. 28).
- **Egypt:** Memphite theology describes the god Ptah creating by thought and spoken word, which offers a partial parallel to creation by command.
- **The heavenly bodies:** On day four the sun and moon are not named, only "the greater light" and "the lesser light" (v. 16). In surrounding cultures the sun and moon were major deities (and the Hebrew words for them resemble divine names). Many interpreters see a quiet polemic here: they are lamps set in place to mark time, not gods to be worshiped.
- **The image of God:** Mesopotamian and Egyptian texts call the *king* the image of a god, and kings set up statues as their "image" in conquered lands. Genesis 1:26–27 gives that royal status to *all* humankind, male and female.

## Reading Approaches (Hermeneutics)

Readers have long disagreed about what kind of text Genesis 1 is, and the chapter supports several careful readings:

- **Literal-chronological:** six consecutive 24-hour days.
- **Day-age:** each "day" is a long period.
- **Literary or framework:** the days are a theological structure (see the Literary Unit tab) rather than a timeline.
- **Functional or cosmic-temple** (John Walton and others): the chapter describes God assigning functions and roles to the cosmos and taking up residence in it on the seventh day, the way a temple is dedicated.

**Latter-day Saint notes:** Abraham 4 calls each period a "time" (Abraham 4:8), and Abraham 5:13 refers to "the Lord's time" being different from human reckoning (see Abraham 3:4). Moses 3:5 adds that God created all things "spiritually" before they were naturally on the earth. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has taken no official position on the age of the earth or the length of the creative periods.

## Why It Matters

Genesis 1 sets out the Bible's foundational claims: one God creates by his word, the created world is repeatedly called "good" (seven times), humankind bears God's image, and the week ends in sacred rest. Later scripture builds directly on it. John 1:1–3 and Hebrews 11:3 return to creation by the word, Moses 2:1 names the Only Begotten as the one through whom God created, and in Moses 2:26 the "Let us" of v. 26 is addressed to him.
